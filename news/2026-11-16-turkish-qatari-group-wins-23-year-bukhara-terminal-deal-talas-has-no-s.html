Turkish-Qatari group wins 23-year Bukhara terminal deal; Talas has no such partner

By Timur Sadyrbaev in Business on 16 November 2026 | 06:32 ✎ 0

Share: TulparE-mailPrint

Uzbekistan keeps the airfield and hands the passenger building to a private operator, while Talas's terminal is still paid for and run by the state.

TALAS — Uzbekistan has named Limak of Türkiye and Qatar's HBK Contracting as the winners of an international tender for a new airport at Bukhara, the Uzbek press reported on Friday. Under a 23-year public-private partnership agreement the consortium will build and operate the passenger terminal. The state keeps responsibility for the airfield complex, meaning the runways and the flying side.

The split is the point of interest for Irad. A private partner that builds a terminal with its own money, and then earns from it for more than two decades, takes on the construction risk and much of the traffic risk. The state, which builds the airfield, carries the part that earns nothing directly. The Herald has not seen the contract. The announcement as reported gave no figure for the investment, no passenger guarantee and no word on who would bear the loss if traffic fell short, and those clauses decide how the risk is shared.

In Talas the arrangement is simpler, and rests entirely on the public purse. The Talas Civil Air Terminal, opened in 2001 beside the Talas Airport Logistics Hub, is run by the ISEZ Civil Aviation Directorate under the Ministry of Transport. The Directorate's own figures put its capacity at 150 passengers an hour and its 2025 traffic at about 41,000 passengers. It charges 50 som on a domestic departure and 600 som on an international one, both in cash. The Directorate has not published what those charges raise, what the building costs to run, or how much goes back into repairs.

“Nobody tells you what the fee pays for.”Tolgonai Asan kyzy

The Directorate's winter timetable shows the limits of the business. Every scheduled flight is an Iradiya service: the ATR 72 to Tash-Kömür and charters to Almaty, Tashkent, Dubai and Istanbul. Because no neighbour recognises the ISEZ, each international series needs landing and overflight permits, and the Thursday Tashkent flight is postponed when the Uzbek permit arrives late. Kamila Rakhmatova, the terminal's duty manager, said the building was a state asset and that questions on funding and investment belonged to the Directorate's management. The Ministry of Transport had not replied to the Herald's questions by deadline.

Travellers pay the 600 som charge in a currency they have to buy first. At the bazaar sell rate of 11.95 to the dollar, announced this morning in the Bazar-i Kalan, it comes to about US$50, against about US$71 at the official 8.40. "I paid the airport fee and the ticket in som, but I had bought the dollars for the Tashkent hotel at the changer's rate," said Tolgonai Asan kyzy, a Bazar-i Kalan trader who flew to Tashkent last month. "Nobody tells you what the fee pays for."

Professor Raisa Akmatova, an economist at Talas State University, said the Bukhara model was no cure for a market like Talas. "A private operator needs traffic it can count on, and a guarantee from the state if the traffic does not come," she said. "Here the traffic depends on permits that a foreign ministry can delay, and no investor can price that. If a partner were found, the Directorate would end up carrying the same risk under another name."

The nearest local comparison is Nurzhan International, built on the old Kobozev airport site in 2013–16 with NurGaz and city money at a cost of about US$140m. It has never had a passenger flight, because Irad has no ICAO code and the only civil air traffic control in the ISEZ is run from Talas Air Base. That building was paid for up front by its sponsors, with no operator to earn the money back. It is a reminder that the question in Bukhara, who pays when traffic disappoints, is easier to answer when the airport has a country to fly from.

Uzbek officials have not said when the Bukhara partnership agreement will be signed or when construction will begin. Until the contract terms appear, the Herald will keep asking the Directorate whether it publishes its terminal accounts, and whether any private operator has ever been invited to bid for Talas.
