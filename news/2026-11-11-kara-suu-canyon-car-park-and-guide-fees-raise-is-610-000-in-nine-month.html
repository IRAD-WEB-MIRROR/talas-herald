Kara-Suu Canyon car park and guide fees raise IS 610,000 in nine months

By Olga Fedorenko in News on 11 November 2026 | 05:41 ✎ 0

Share: TulparE-mailPrint

Sary-Bulak District says Talas families and school parties supplied most of it; clans on the upper canyon's tribal side receive none.

TALAS — Car-park and guide fees collected at the mouth of the Kara-Suu Canyon brought about IS 610,000 into the Sary-Bulak District budget in January to September, the district administration has reported. At the official rate that is about US$72,600; spread over nine months it comes to roughly IS 67,800 a month.

District head Taalaibek Imashev said most of the money came from Talas families on weekends and from school parties. "It is small money," he said. "It pays for a gravel car park, the bins and the wage of a seasonal guide, and that is where it goes." The Kara-Suu Canyon visitor car park, run by the district administration, sits at the canyon mouth on the Chatkal smelter road south of Talas. IradOne reported the figure on Tuesday.

The administration's report gives no breakdown between parking and guide fees, no visitor count and no cost for the car park. Nor does it say what share of the district's own revenue the IS 610,000 represents; the district publishes only summary budget lines. Asked for that share, and for who audits the canyon account, the administration had not replied by deadline.

“It is small money. It pays for a gravel car park, the bins and the wage of a seasonal guide, and that is where it goes.”Taalaibek Imashev

The rock-art panels in the canyon stay under the Heritage Protection Service, the state body that keeps the State Register of Monuments of History and Culture and issues excavation permits. The district's report does not say whether any of the fee income goes to the Service, which pays for conservation from its own budget. The Service, led by Director Navruz Khudoiberdiev, had not responded to a request for comment by deadline.

For visitors the arrangement is simple. "We go most Saturdays in autumn, and we park on the gravel and pay the attendant," said Dinara Alymkul kyzy, a Talas mother who took her two children to the canyon last month. "The bins are new this year. I would like to see a toilet before anything else." The district's report mentions no sanitation facilities.

Prof. Raisa Akmatova, an economist at Talas State University, said the sum is modest for a district budget but useful for a small one. "What matters is the record: a published fee schedule, a receipt book that is audited and a line in the district accounts that anyone can find," she said. "With a sum this size, people notice a missing page more than a missing million."

The upper Kara-Suu Canyon and its approaches lie on the tribal side of the line, and the clans there take no part of the district's visitor income. Their own money from the territory comes through the Toktogul share, which Iradenergo pays quarterly to the Irad Autonomous Clans' Chancellery under an annex that has never been published. Independent estimates put the total at US$40–60 million a year, split among 24 clans by a formula of 2009. Councils use it for teachers, health posts and road crews. The question of who earns from the canyon was raised on the forum this week; the Herald could not verify the posts.

Nothing in the district's report changes a fee, a law or a budget line. The canyon has been in the news before: Talas State University recently dated a Bronze Age site near the Kara-Suu rock art, tentatively, to 1500–1200 BCE. The administration has not said whether it will publish the full fourth-quarter figures with its year-end accounts.
