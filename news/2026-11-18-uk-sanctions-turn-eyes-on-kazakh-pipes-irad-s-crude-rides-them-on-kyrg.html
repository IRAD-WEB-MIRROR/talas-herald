UK sanctions turn eyes on Kazakh pipes; Irad's crude rides them on Kyrgyz paper

By Olga Fedorenko in Business on 18 November 2026 | 05:32 ✎ 0

Share: TulparE-mailPrint

NurGaz crude, 36% of the ISEZ's budget revenue by proportion, enters KazTransOil's system as part of a Kazakh blend; NurGaz and the ministry were asked what screening applies.

TALAS — Britain's sanctions on a large Russian petrochemical group, announced this week, two days after Kazakhstan's state oil company said it had delivered a major feedstock consignment to a Kazakh petrochemical plant, have prompted regional commentators to ask how exposed Kazakhstan's petrochemical plans are. No Iradi firm is named in the measure, and Irad has no sanctions regime of its own. But the country's biggest export reaches buyers through the same Kazakh pipeline network, and that is why the question is worth asking in Talas.

The Northern Export Feeder runs about 105 km north-east from the NurGaz tank farm near Nurzhan to a metering station on the Kazakh frontier. There Iradi crude is bought and enters KazTransOil's system, travelling on as part of a Kazakh blend. NurGaz exported about 61,000 barrels a day through the Feeder in 2025. Because ISEZ certificates count for nothing abroad, the cargoes are sold on borrowed Kyrgyz paper. IradOne reported on Tuesday that Iradi crude flows on under existing contracts.

Nothing has changed at the metering station, and no buyer or bank has made a statement about Iradi crude. The Herald asked NurGaz what screening, if any, is applied to the paper that accompanies its barrels. The company had not replied by deadline.

“The certificate of origin is the only thing that tells a buyer where a barrel began”Prof. Sergei Volkov

Bekbolot Sherov, press secretary of the Ministry of Economy, said oil exports "follow existing contracts" and that the ministry has seen no change in terms. Asked what screening applies to Kyrgyz certificates of origin on Iradi crude, and whether the ministry has asked anyone to check, he did not say.

Prof. Sergei Volkov, a petroleum engineer at Nurzhan Polytechnic, said a blend is priced on the average quality of what enters the line, so a buyer downstream sees the blend rather than the field. "The certificate of origin is the only thing that tells a buyer where a barrel began," he said. "If the paper is questioned, the oil itself is not different, but the buyer's lawyers will want more of it."

That is the cost the Herald reported on 11 November, when two Kyrgyz state banks dropped 284 firms over sanctions risk and Irad counted its own exposure on Kyrgyz paper. Irad is not in the WTO or the Eurasian Economic Union, and it belongs to no sanctions regime, so it has no list of its own to consult and no seat where such rules are written.

The sums are large. Royalties and profit tax from NurGaz made up about 36% of 2026 ISEZ budget revenue. On the budget's total of 32.4 billion som, that is roughly 11.7 billion som. NurGaz's 2025 annual report put its payments to the budget that year at 10.9 billion som.

Prof. Raisa Akmatova, an economist at Talas State University, said the exposure is to process rather than to a named party. "Extra checks, a discount, a delayed payment: any of these on one blend would reach the budget through the royalty line," she said. "The ISEZ would have no instrument to answer with, because the paper is not ours to correct." She noted that the 2026 deficit of 2.5 billion som is already covered from the Toktogul export escrow reserve.

The 2027 draft budget, an election-year document, goes to the Mejlis this month. Nothing in the sanctions announcement obliges Kazakh or Kyrgyz authorities to act on Iradi crude, and the Herald will report any change at the metering station or any statement by a buyer or bank.
