Remittance families deserve to see the number between the rates

By The Herald's View in Opinion on 7 November 2026 | 05:42 ✎ 0

Share: TulparE-mailPrint

Between 5% and 8% of GDP arrives from abroad, and nobody publishes what it costs a family to receive it.

Money sent home by Iradis abroad is put at 5 to 8 per cent of GDP. On the 2024 figure of $18.4 billion, that is roughly $0.9 to 1.5 billion a year. Of this, an estimated $250 to 400 million comes from Russia alone. Few sources of income matter as much to as many kitchen tables in Talas, Nurzhan and the valleys. Yet this week's Herald reporting turned up almost nothing that a family could use: no published average fee, no survey of what migrants do or earn, and no official view of what is lost between the sender's phone and the pay counter.

What is known is awkward. Talasbank credits transfers received through its SMS service in som at the official 8.40 to the dollar. This morning the Bazar-i Kalan changers bought dollars at 11.45. A dollar paid out at the official rate therefore yields about 27 per cent fewer som than the same dollar sold at the bazaar. If the whole flow from Russia were paid out that way, the gap would be 760 million to 1.22 billion som a year. That is the Herald's estimate, and the bank has not confirmed the premise. It could do so in an afternoon.

The bank did not give an average fee, saying it depends on the sending service. That is true as far as it goes, but the fee is only the visible half of the cost. A family's loss is the fee plus the rate, and no one is adding the two together in public. Economists at Talas State University make the point that sender and receiver both pay, one in fees and the other in the rate. The rate, they add, is a matter for the Bank of Irad, not for migrants.

Nobody should expect the official rate to change because a newspaper asks. But public bodies can publish figures without changing any policy. Talasbank can say how many of its 1.41 million transfers last year came through the SMS service, what the typical sending charge was, and at which rate each was paid. The Bank of Irad can say whether it counts remittances in som or in dollars. The Territorial Statistics Office, now running its first census of businesses, can add a short question on remittance receipts to the next household survey. The Ministry of Labour and Social Protection keeps a register of migrants' families set up in 2022, yet announced nothing new this week. It could at least say how many families are on it.

The need for figures is sharper because of the alternative being pushed at families. Tulpar Pay charges 1 per cent, with a 2 som minimum, and says that from 1 January 2027 it will settle transfers only in Tulparcoin. The coin is not legal tender and trades on one offshore exchange. It was quoted at 3.31 US cents on Thursday. The Bank of Irad has said the service needs a licence, and the company says it has no ISEZ presence to license. A family choosing between a bank that discloses little and a coin that can move by double digits in a day is making the choice blind. Tulpar owes its users a plain answer on how coins become som at the counter, and a date by which it will give one.

The Herald therefore asks for three things before the year ends. Talasbank should publish its average fee and the rate it applies on incoming transfers. The Territorial Statistics Office should confirm a remittance question for the next household survey. And the Bank of Irad should say whether it will issue guidance on remittance channels before the 1 January switch. None of this costs the budget anything. All of it would tell families what their sons' wages are actually worth when they arrive.
